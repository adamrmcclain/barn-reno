Barn Reno · Step 1

Walls & Floor — Done Right

Clean · Repair · Stain · Seal · In that order

1

Assess before you touch anything

Walk the space systematically and log what you're dealing with. This prevents buying the wrong products and starting steps in the wrong order. Budget 1–2 hours.

DO
Check for rot and soft spots on the floor
Floor · ~20 min
Walk every plank slowly, pressing with your heel. Soft, spongy, or springy areas mean rot beneath — these need replacement or sister boards before anything else. Pay extra attention within 3 ft of the double doors (water intrusion over the years) and along the exterior walls.
  • Probe suspicious spots with a screwdriver — if it sinks in easily, the wood is compromised
  • Mark bad boards with painter's tape as you go
  • Check the substructure (joists) below through any gaps you can see
!
Test for lead paint on the walls
Walls · 10 min
Barns built or painted before 1978 may have lead-based paint. A swab test kit ($10–15 at any hardware store) gives results in 30 seconds. If positive, the paint is stable when sealed over — but you can't sand or dry-scrape it. This changes your cleaning and prep approach significantly.

3M LeadCheck Swabs Pro-Lab Lead Test Kit
DO
Identify what's on the walls — paint, stain, or raw wood
Walls · 15 min
This matters because raw wood, old paint, and old stain all need different prep. Try these tests:
  • Water bead test: sprinkle water on the wall — if it beads, there's a sealer or paint on it already
  • Scratch test: lightly scratch with a coin — if a colored layer comes off, it's painted
  • Raw wood will absorb the water immediately and look darker where wet
Most old barn board is raw or has a long-faded wash — which is ideal, as it'll absorb stain beautifully.
DO
Check for active moisture, mold, or mildew
Walls + Floor · 20 min
Look for dark staining, white efflorescence (mineral deposits), or musty smell concentrated in corners and along the foundation wall. Any active moisture source must be fixed before sealing — trapping moisture under a sealer causes it to lift and peel within months.
  • Check where the stone foundation meets the wood floor — common trouble spot
  • Look up at the roof line for water staining on the rafters
  • Note which direction the prevailing weather comes from (usually the corner with the most weathering)
TIP
Take photos of every problem area
All · 10 min
Document with your phone before cleaning anything. You'll want reference photos to show a contractor if something's worse than expected, and it's satisfying to have a true before-and-after when you're done.
2

Clean — thoroughly, and in the right order

Top to bottom, walls before floor. Cleaning activates or reveals wood grain and is the single most important factor in how well stain and sealer adhere.

DO
Clear the space completely first
~2–4 hrs depending on clutter
Everything out. You cannot properly clean or stain around furniture, and moving things mid-process means tracking dust and debris back onto clean surfaces. Rent a dumpster or schedule a haul-away day first.
DO
Knock down cobwebs and loose debris — walls and ceiling first
Walls/ceiling · 1 hr
Use a stiff broom or brush on an extension pole. Work top to bottom — rafters, then upper walls, then lower walls. Wear a dust mask (N95 minimum — old barns have decades of particulates). Let the dust settle before sweeping the floor.

N95 respirator Extension brush
DO
Wash the walls with a wood cleaner or diluted TSP
Walls · 2–3 hrs
TSP (trisodium phosphate) is the gold standard for old wood. It removes grime, mildew, and old oily residue that would prevent stain absorption. Mix at the label ratio — usually 1/4 cup per gallon of warm water.
  • Apply with a stiff-bristle brush, scrubbing with the grain
  • Work in 4×4 ft sections; don't let it dry on the surface
  • Rinse with clean water and a damp sponge — you don't need to drench it
  • Let dry fully — 24–48 hrs minimum before any stain or sealer
If lead paint is present: skip scrubbing, wipe down gently with a damp cloth, and use an encapsulating primer instead of stain.

TSP Substitute (no-rinse) Savogran TSP
DO
Sweep, then vacuum the floor
Floor · 45 min
Sweep first to get the bulk. Follow with a shop vac to get into the gaps between planks — this debris will show through a clear sealer and will also prevent proper adhesion. A leaf blower through the open doors first can help move the big stuff.
DO
Scrub the floor with a deck cleaner
Floor · 2 hrs + dry time
A deck and fence cleaner (oxygen bleach type) is perfect for old barn floors — it lifts grime, brightens gray wood, kills mildew spores, and opens the grain for better sealer penetration.
  • Mix per label instructions (usually 1 scoop per gallon)
  • Apply with a stiff deck brush, scrub with the grain
  • Let dwell 10–15 min, then rinse — mop or bucket + squeegee out the doors
  • Allow 48 hrs of dry time before staining or sealing — do not rush this
Cabot Deck Cleaner Olympic Deck Cleaner OxiClean Outdoor
!
Do not sand the floor at this stage
Floor
Old barn floor boards are likely uneven — sanding before assessing will tear up the softer weathered grain that gives the wood its character, and you risk going through thin spots. Clean and assess first. If you decide to sand later (optional for Plan B), do it after repair, not before cleaning.
3

Repair what needs fixing

Now that it's clean, you can properly see what needs work. Address structural problems before applying any finish — stain and sealer won't hide damage, they'll highlight it.

DO
Replace or sister rotted floor boards
Floor · varies
Any board that failed the screwdriver test needs to go. You have two options:
  • Replace: Pull the board, replace with reclaimed wood of matching thickness. Reclaimed lumber from a salvage yard will age-match better than new wood.
  • Sister: If the joist below is sound but the board is thin, sister a new board alongside — overlap by at least 16 inches and screw into the joist.
Resist the urge to just lay plywood over bad spots — it will look patchy and create trip hazards.
DO
Re-nail or screw loose boards and squeaky planks
Floor · 1–2 hrs
Walk the whole floor again after cleaning and mark every squeak. Drive 3" coarse-thread screws through the face of loose boards into the joist below. Countersink the heads so they sit flush or just below the surface.

Don't use finish nails — use structural screws. A squeaky floor in an entertainment space will drive you crazy.

3" Torx deck screws
DO
Fill large gaps and cracks in the floor (optional)
Floor · 1 hr + dry time
Gaps between planks are part of the character — don't try to fill them all. But gaps wider than 1/2 inch are dust traps and can catch chair legs. Fill those with a flexible wood filler or rope caulk that can move with seasonal expansion.

Do not use rigid wood filler in wide gaps — it will crack when the wood moves in winter.

Dap Plastic Wood (flexible) Sashco Log Builder Caulk
DO
Re-secure loose wall boards and fill gaps
Walls · 1–2 hrs
Any boards that are pulling away from the studs get screwed back in — use 2.5" screws driven at a slight angle if the original nails have pulled out. Gaps in the exterior walls should be filled from the inside with backer rod + caulk to stop drafts and critters.
TIP
Embrace imperfection — it's the aesthetic
You're not trying to make this look like a new floor. Uneven boards, nail holes, old stains that won't come out, slight warping — these are features. Fix the structural stuff, leave the character. A too-perfect floor kills the rustic vibe instantly.
4

Stain — the most visible decision you'll make

Color choice will define the whole room. The key insight: old barn wood is already dark and weathered — most stains will go darker than the swatch shows. Always test on the actual surface first.

!
Always test on a hidden section first
Before anything
Old weathered wood absorbs stain unevenly. Buy a small sample, test on a 1×2 ft section behind where the bar will go, and live with it for a day in different lighting conditions. What looks great at noon may look completely different under evening string lights — which is the condition that actually matters for an entertaining space.
DO
Apply stain to walls with a brush, not a roller
Walls · 3–4 hrs (front room)
Barn board has irregular grooves and rough texture — a roller skips the low spots. Use a 3–4 inch natural bristle brush and work with the grain. Wipe off excess after 5–10 minutes (before it gets tacky) with a lint-free rag — this gives an even tone and prevents lap marks.
  • Work one board at a time, end to end
  • Don't stop mid-board — you'll get a visible lap line
  • Have extra rags ready — old wood can absorb a lot quickly
DO
Apply stain to the floor with a long-handle applicator
Floor · 2–3 hrs + dry time
Start at the far corner from the door and work toward the exit — you cannot walk on fresh stain. Use a long-handled foam applicator pad or a lambswool applicator (better for rough wood). Apply thin coats — two thin coats always beats one heavy coat.
  • Work with the grain, 3–4 boards at a time
  • Wipe up excess within 5 minutes
  • Allow 4–6 hours between coats, 24 hours before sealing
Minwax foam applicator pad Lambswool floor applicator
TIP
Match floor and wall stain — but go lighter on walls
If using the same stain family, dilute the wall stain 20–30% with mineral spirits (oil-based) or water (water-based). The floor gets the full color. Walls slightly lighter creates depth and visual separation, and prevents the room feeling like a dark box.
5

Seal — different products for floor vs walls

This is what protects everything. The floor needs a tough, wear-resistant finish. The walls need something that lets the wood breathe and keeps the matte rustic look. Don't use the same product for both.

DO
Floor: use a water-based polyurethane — satin sheen
Floor · 2 coats + dry time
Water-based poly dries clear (oil-based yellows over time, killing the natural wood tone), dries faster, and has lower VOCs in an unventilated space. Satin sheen is the right call — gloss looks too finished and modern, matte doesn't hold up to traffic.
  • Apply with a synthetic-bristle applicator pad on a pole
  • Thin first coat 10% with water for better penetration
  • Lightly sand with 220-grit between coats (tack cloth after)
  • Minimum 2 coats; 3 if the space will see heavy foot traffic and spills
  • Full cure: 7 days before placing furniture
Minwax Water-Based Poly (Satin) Bona Traffic HD Varathane Crystal Clear
DO
Walls: use a penetrating wood sealer, not poly
Walls · 1–2 coats
Poly on rough barn board walls looks plasticky and will crack as the wood moves seasonally. Use a penetrating oil sealer or a matte interior wood sealer instead — it soaks in rather than sitting on top, preserves the texture, and won't peel.
  • Apply with a brush, working it into the grain
  • Wipe off excess after 20–30 min
  • One coat is usually sufficient on already-gray weathered wood
  • Re-apply every 3–5 years as needed
Waterlox Original Sealer Cabot Australian Timber Oil Ready Seal (matte)
!
Ventilate during application — and for 48 hrs after
Critical
Even low-VOC water-based products off-gas in a closed space. Open all doors and windows, run a box fan exhausting outward, and don't sleep in the space for at least 48 hours after the final coat. Oil-based products need even more ventilation — 72+ hours with active airflow.
TIP
Seal the ceiling/rafters last, or skip it entirely
Rafters don't need a finish coat — they're not touched and the raw, dusty look is part of the aesthetic. If you want to clean them up, a single coat of matte sealer applied with a brush is enough. Don't poly the rafters — that sheen up high will look very wrong under string lights.
✓
Final check: the floor test
After full cure
After 7 days of cure time, do a water bead test in a few spots. Water should bead and sit on the surface — if it soaks in, the sealer didn't fully bond and you need another coat in that area. Also slide a piece of furniture across the floor — you shouldn't hear any dragging or feel any stickiness.

◷

Realistic weekend-by-weekend timeline

This is the minimum time needed — rushing dry and cure times is the most common mistake. Plan for 4–5 weekends total, with gaps in between for drying.

Weekend 1 — Saturday
Full cleanout + assessment
Everything out of the space. Lead test. Walk the floor. Mark problem areas. Take photos. Haul debris. ~6–8 hrs.
Weekend 1 — Sunday
Wall cleaning (TSP) + ceiling sweep
Knock down cobwebs, scrub walls with TSP, allow to dry. Don't touch the floor yet. Open doors, leave ventilating overnight.
Weekday gap — 48 hrs dry time
Walls drying
Do nothing. This is load-bearing waiting time.
Weekend 2 — Saturday
Floor repair + floor cleaning
Re-secure loose boards, replace any rotted planks, fill wide gaps. Then scrub floor with deck cleaner. Squeegee out the door.
Weekend 2 — Sunday
Buy stain + test patches
Buy 2–3 stain samples. Apply test patches on the wall and floor in an inconspicuous spot. Let dry overnight and evaluate in the evening under the actual lighting conditions you'll have.
Weekday gap — 48 hrs dry time
Floor drying + stain decision
Look at the test patches at night with string lights. Make your stain decision. Buy full cans.
Weekend 3 — Saturday
Stain the walls
Full day staining walls. Start at back and work toward door. Wipe excess as you go. Leave doors open to ventilate overnight.
Weekend 3 — Sunday
Stain the floor — coat 1
Start far corner, work toward door. Don't walk on it. Let dry 24 hrs minimum.
Weekday gap — 24 hrs
Floor stain coat 1 drying
No traffic. Ventilate.
Weekend 4 — Saturday
Floor stain coat 2 + wall sealer
Second coat of stain on floor. While waiting, apply penetrating sealer to walls.
Weekend 4 — Sunday
Floor poly coat 1
Light 220-grit sand, tack cloth, apply first coat of water-based poly. Back corner to door. 24 hr dry.
Weekend 5
Floor poly coats 2 (+ 3) · Done
Sand lightly between coats. Apply final coat(s). 7-day cure before furniture goes in. You're done with Step 1.
Day 7 after final coat
Move in the furniture
Do the water bead test first. If it passes — time to hang string lights and build the bar.
Total elapsed time: ~5–6 weeks from first cleanout to furniture moving in. You can compress this if you have full weeks available, but the dry and cure times are non-negotiable. The floor will be damaged if you put furniture on uncured poly.
