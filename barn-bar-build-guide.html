The Bar Build

Reclaimed wood · Drop-in sink · RV portable water · Intermediate DIY

✦

What you're building

A wall-mounted reclaimed wood bar — roughly 8 ft wide, 42 inches tall (bar height), 20 inches deep. A drop-in utility sink fed by a self-contained RV-style fresh water tank with a 12V pump. Open bottle shelving above on the wall. The whole thing is built into the left wall of the entertaining space.

Wall-mounted vs freestanding: Building this directly against the left wall (screwing ledger boards into the studs) is the right approach. It's more stable than a freestanding bar, it uses the wall as the back panel, and it won't shift or tip. Locate the studs before anything else.

Your dad's instinct was exactly right — this is the standard setup for off-grid outdoor kitchens and bar builds.

1

Build the frame first — everything else hangs off it

The frame is a simple box structure: ledger boards screwed into the wall studs, vertical legs at the front, horizontal rails connecting them. Reclaimed 2×4s or rough-sawn 2×6s work perfectly.

DO
Locate and mark all studs on the left wall
~20 min
The ledger boards — horizontal lumber screwed into the wall that the bar top and shelf rest on — need to hit studs. In a barn, studs may not be standard 16" on center. Use a stud finder, or knock and listen, or probe with a small nail. Mark every stud from floor to about 5 ft up across the full 8 ft section.

You need at least 4 stud connections for the lower ledger and 4 for the upper ledger to hold bar weight confidently. Stud finder ($15–25)
DO
Mount the lower ledger board at 40" off the floor
~1 hr
The lower ledger sits at 40" — the bar top will rest on top of it, bringing the finished height to 42". Use a 2×4 or 2×6 reclaimed board, level it carefully, and drive 3" structural screws into every stud you can hit.
  • Use a 4 ft level — any tilt shows in the finished top
  • Pre-drill to avoid splitting reclaimed lumber
  • Minimum 2 screws per stud connection
  • Check level again after final tightening — it can shift slightly
2×6 ledger board 3" structural screws
DO
Add front legs and connect with horizontal rails
~2 hrs
Two or three front vertical legs (4×4 posts work well, or doubled 2×4s) run from floor to 40". These carry the front edge of the bar top. Connect them to the ledger with horizontal 2×4 rails — one near the top and one near the floor for rigidity.
  • Space front legs no more than 48" apart — closer if you can
  • Toenail or use metal joist hangers to connect rails to legs
  • Check for square before final tightening — measure diagonals
  • The lower rail near the floor creates a shelf for the water tank and grey bucket to sit on
4×4 posts or 2×4 doubled Joist hanger brackets
TIP
Leave the sink bay open on the bottom
Wherever the sink will sit — plan that section of the lower frame to be open and accessible from the front. You need to be able to reach in to swap the water tank, empty the grey bucket, and check the pump. A simple curtain or a hinged reclaimed wood panel covers the opening when not in use.
!
Reclaimed lumber needs to be flat and straight for the frame
The face boards and top surface can have character — checking, knots, rough texture are great. But the structural frame boards need to be reasonably straight. A bowed ledger means a bowed bar top. Sort through your reclaimed pile and use the straightest boards for the frame; save the most characterful pieces for the visible face and top.
2

The bar top — the most visible piece

A few good options depending on what you find. Butcher block is the easiest to work with and looks great. A wide reclaimed slab is more dramatic but harder to source. Both work for this build.

DO
Choose your top material — three real options
Option 1 — Butcher block: $80–180 for an 8 ft section at IKEA (Numerar) or any home center. Easy to cut, sands beautifully, takes stain well, and pairs perfectly with reclaimed wood. Easiest option for an intermediate builder.

Option 2 — Reclaimed wide plank: Source a single wide board (12–14" wide) from a salvage yard or old barn flooring. More character and authenticity, but you'll need to flatten and joint it if it's warped. More work, more reward.

Option 3 — Board-and-batten top: Edge-glue several narrower reclaimed boards together, biscuit-joined or doweled, fill gaps with epoxy, sand flat. Fully DIY, uses whatever lumber you have, looks fantastic when done well.

IKEA Numerar (butcher block) Home Depot butcher block countertop
DO
Cut the sink opening before mounting the top
~1 hr
Always cut the sink hole before the top is mounted — it's much easier to work on a flat surface than leaning over a mounted bar. Follow the template that comes with your sink (most drop-in sinks include one). Use a jigsaw.
  • Drill a starter hole inside the cut line before using the jigsaw
  • Cut slightly inside the template line — you can always trim more, you can't add back
  • Support the cutout piece from below so it doesn't fall and split when you finish the cut
  • Test-fit the sink before final mounting
Jigsaw with wood blade Drill + 1" spade bit for starter hole
DO
Sand, stain, and seal the top before mounting
~2 hrs + dry time
Much easier to finish the top on sawhorses before mounting. Sand to 150 grit, stain to match or complement your floor (or go natural — butcher block tops look great with a clear food-safe oil), then seal with a waterproof finish around the sink area specifically.
  • Use food-safe mineral oil or cutting board oil if going natural/clear
  • Use a spar urethane or bar top epoxy if you want maximum water resistance
  • Apply extra sealer around the sink cutout edges — end grain absorbs water
  • Let fully cure before mounting — 24–48 hrs for oil, 72 hrs for poly
Waterlox Original (food-safe) Bar top epoxy (maximum protection) Spar urethane (middle ground)
DO
Mount the top — screws up through the frame
~30 min
Set the top onto the ledger and front rails, check it's flush and level, then drive 2.5" screws up through the frame into the underside of the top. Don't go through the top surface. 6–8 screws across the length is plenty. Apply a bead of construction adhesive along the ledger first for extra bond — optional but good. 2.5" screws driven from below Liquid Nails (optional adhesive)
3

The RV water system — simpler than it sounds

Three components: a fresh water tank, a 12V demand pump, and a faucet. The pump only runs when the faucet is open (demand pump = it senses pressure drop and kicks on automatically). Total install time is about 2 hours.

Fresh water tank

A 5 or 7 gallon RV/marine fresh water tank. Sits under the bar on the lower shelf. Fills via a screw cap on top — just pour water in from a jug or garden hose. A 7-gallon tank holds enough for a full evening of use.

  • Reliance Aqua-Tainer (7 gal) — $25
  • Or a dedicated RV portable tank — $30–50

12V demand pump

Shurflo 2088 is the standard — it's what's in most RVs and off-grid setups. Runs on 12V DC. Connect to a small sealed lead-acid battery (rechargeable) or to a 12V adapter plugged into an outlet once you have power.

  • Shurflo 2088-554-144 — ~$50–65
  • 12V 7Ah sealed battery — $20–30
  • Or 12V wall adapter if you have an outlet

Faucet

Any standard RV or bar faucet with 3/8" push-connect or barbed fittings. Single-handle is easiest. RV faucets are cheap ($20–40) and come with the right fitting sizes for 1/2" tubing.

  • Dura Faucet RV bar faucet — $25–35
  • Or a standard kitchen faucet — needs adapter fittings

Grey water bucket

The drain from the sink goes into a 5-gallon bucket under the bar. Just a simple PVC drain tailpiece, a short section of 1.5" flexible drain hose, and the bucket. Empty after each use — takes 30 seconds.

  • 5-gal bucket with lid — $8
  • PVC drain kit — $12–18
  • Flexible drain hose — $8–12

Installation steps

DO
Mount the pump to a board under the bar
~30 min
Screw the Shurflo pump to a small piece of plywood or directly to the lower shelf rail. It needs to be accessible but secured — vibration from the pump can walk it around if it's just sitting loose.
  • Mount near the fresh water tank, not the drain side
  • Leave enough slack in the tubing to pull the tank out for refilling
  • The pump has an inlet and outlet marked — inlet from tank, outlet to faucet
DO
Run 1/2" tubing: tank → pump → faucet
~45 min
Use 1/2" ID clear vinyl tubing — sold by the foot at hardware stores. Use barbed fittings and hose clamps at every connection. Route the tubing up through a small hole in the bar top near where the faucet sits.
  • Keep tubing runs short and direct — less pressure drop
  • Secure tubing with zip ties or cable staples so it doesn't flop around
  • Apply thread tape (Teflon tape) to all threaded fittings before assembly
  • Test all connections before closing up the bar front
1/2" clear vinyl tubing Barbed fittings + hose clamps Teflon tape
DO
Wire the pump to a battery or 12V adapter
~30 min
The Shurflo 2088 has two wires — red (positive) and black (negative). Connect to a 12V sealed battery with ring terminals and a small inline fuse (the pump draws about 5A max, use a 10A fuse). Add a simple rocker switch in the line so you can kill the pump when the bar isn't in use.

Once you have a proper electrical circuit in the barn (Plan B electrical work), you can swap the battery for a 12V DC wall adapter and never worry about charging it. 10A inline fuse holder 12V rocker switch Ring terminal connectors
DO
Install the drain — simple gravity setup
~30 min
Drop-in bar sinks come with a drain hole — install a standard 1.5" basket strainer drain. Connect 1.5" flexible drain hose (or PVC elbow + pipe) from the drain down to the grey water bucket below. The bucket just needs to be lower than the sink drain — gravity does the rest.
  • Make sure the grey bucket has a lid to reduce evaporation and smell
  • Put the bucket on a small tray in case of drips
  • Label it clearly so guests don't mistake it for something else
TIP
Winterize the system if the barn isn't heated in winter
If the barn will go unheated in winter, drain the tank, run the pump briefly to clear the lines, and leave the faucet open to drain any residual water. A frozen pump or burst line is a $60 fix but an avoidable headache. Takes 5 minutes each fall.
4

Wall shelving — the bottle display above the bar

Three shelves above the bar, running the full 8 ft. These are purely decorative/functional — they hold bottles, glasses, and whatever else. Simple floating shelves on wall-mounted brackets.

DO
Mount a French cleat system on the wall
~1.5 hrs
A French cleat is two boards cut at 45° angles — one screws to the wall, the other to the shelf. The shelf just hooks on. Advantages: dead simple, incredibly strong, and you can reposition shelves without any new holes in the wall.
  • Rip a 2×4 at 45° on a table saw or circular saw with a rip guide
  • Screw the wall piece into studs — every stud across the full 8 ft
  • The shelf piece attaches to the underside of each shelf board
  • Start the lowest cleat at 54–56" off the floor (12–14" above the bar top)
  • Space subsequent cleats 12–14" apart for each shelf above
French cleat system 3" screws into studs
DO
Cut and finish the shelf boards
~1 hr
Shelf boards should be 8–10" deep (deep enough for a bottle, not so deep they crowd the bar) and 8 ft long. Reclaimed 1×10 or 1×12 is perfect. Sand lightly — just enough to remove splinters — and apply the same stain you used on the bar top for consistency. Seal with a matte or satin finish.

The front edge of each shelf can be left raw and rough for character, or given a slight chamfer with a block plane for a cleaner look. Either works.
TIP
Add a small lip to the front edge of shelves
A 1×1" or 1×2" strip of wood glued and nailed to the front underside of each shelf creates a lip that keeps bottles from sliding off if someone bumps the shelf. Optional but smart in an entertaining space. Takes 20 minutes and $10 in lumber.
TIP
Add under-shelf lighting between cleats
A strip of warm LED tape light on the underside of the middle and upper shelves, pointed down at the bottles, looks absolutely incredible under string lights and creates the backlit bar atmosphere. Peel-and-stick LED strips are $15–25 for 16 ft and run on USB or 12V — same battery as your pump if you want to keep it off-grid. Govee or Kasa LED strip (warm white 2700K) USB power bank (if no outlet yet)
5

Face boards, details & finishing touches

The frame is hidden behind face boards — reclaimed lumber cut to cover the front and sides. This is where the bar goes from looking like a construction project to looking like it was always there.

DO
Clad the front frame with reclaimed face boards
~2 hrs
Cut reclaimed 1×6 or 1×8 boards to height (from floor to just below the bar top edge) and nail or screw them to the front rails. You don't need them to be perfectly tight — slight gaps are part of the character. Run them vertically for a traditional look, or horizontal for something more modern.
  • Vertical boards with slight gaps = classic barn look
  • Pre-finish the face boards before nailing — easier to get into edges
  • Use a nail gun with 2" finish nails for a clean look, or hand-nail for authenticity
  • The sink access bay gets a curtain rod + canvas drop cloth, or a simple hinged panel
DO
Add a bar rail (foot rest) along the bottom front
~30 min
A horizontal pipe or wooden rail at about 8–10" off the floor running the length of the bar is the traditional foot rail for bar stools. It's one of those details that makes the bar feel like a real bar.
  • Simplest: a 1.5" black iron pipe, threaded, with floor flanges at each end — $30–45 total
  • Or a natural round branch/dowel mounted on wall-mount brackets for a more rustic feel
  • Mount at the base of the face board, centered on the stool spacing
1.5" black iron pipe + floor flanges
DO
Caulk the top-to-wall joint and seal any gaps
~30 min
Where the bar top meets the barn wall, run a bead of paintable caulk — this stops water, critters, and drafts from getting behind the bar. Color-match to your stain. Also caulk around the sink with waterproof silicone — 100% silicone, not paintable caulk, for the sink perimeter. GE Silicone II (clear) for sink Paintable caulk (color-matched) for wall joint
TIP
The small details make the biggest difference
A few finishing moves that elevate the whole bar: a magnetic bottle opener mounted on the face board, two or three hooks underneath for bar towels, a small reclaimed wood menu board or chalkboard mounted on the wall nearby, and a vintage-style bar mat on the counter. None of these cost more than $30 total but they signal that the space was designed, not just built.
