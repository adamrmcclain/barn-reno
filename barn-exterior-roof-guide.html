Exterior is a fundamentally different job

The products, process, and stakes are all different from the interior work. The biggest mistake people make is treating them the same way. Here's what changes.

Interior (Step 1 — done)

  • Controlled environment, no weather
  • Goal: aesthetics + dust sealing
  • Penetrating sealer or poly finish
  • Matte or satin sheen acceptable
  • Clean with TSP, no pressure washing
  • Stain color is a style choice
  • Reapply every 5–10 years
  • Failure = looks bad

Exterior (Step 2 — now)

  • Exposed to rain, UV, freeze/thaw
  • Goal: weatherproofing + wood preservation
  • Exterior-rated penetrating stain only
  • Flat/matte finish only — no sheen
  • Pressure wash or stiff brush + cleaner
  • Stain color affects curb appeal
  • Reapply every 2–4 years
  • Failure = rot, structural damage
Never use interior products on the exterior. Interior sealers and poly finishes are not formulated to handle UV, moisture cycling, or temperature swings. They'll peel within a season and leave the wood less protected than if you'd done nothing.
Sequence matters: Roof before walls, always. Any roof work — cleaning, sealing, patching — will drip rust remover, sealant, and debris down the walls. Do it first so the walls start clean.
Weather window: Exterior stain needs 2–3 dry days minimum — no rain during application and 48 hrs after. Temperature 50–90°F. In Kentucky, aim for late spring or early fall. Avoid direct midday sun on the walls when applying — the stain dries too fast and streaks.
1

Assess the full exterior — roof first, then walls

You need to be on or very close to the roof for this. Do it on a dry day with good light. Bring a flashlight, a screwdriver, binoculars for spots you can't reach, and a notepad.

Roof assessment

DO
Walk the roof if safe — or use a ladder and binoculars
Roof · 30–45 min
Corrugated metal roofs are walkable but slippery when damp or mossy. Use rubber-soled shoes, step on the ridges not the valleys, and have someone spotting. If the roof pitch is steep or you're not comfortable, a good ladder and binoculars get you 80% of the way there.
  • Look for lifted, buckled, or missing panels
  • Check every fastener (screws or nails) — lifted heads mean the panel is pulling away
  • Look for gaps at the ridge cap (top peak) — this is the most common leak point
  • Check the eaves and overhangs where panels meet the fascia
DO
Categorize rust: surface, moderate, or through-rust
Roof · 20 min
Not all rust is the same. The treatment depends entirely on how deep it goes:
  • Surface rust: Orange/brown discoloration but metal is still solid. Scratch with a coin — solid beneath. Treat and seal, no replacement needed.
  • Moderate rust: Pitting visible, surface feels rough or flaky. Metal still intact but thinning. Treat aggressively, seal with rust-encapsulating primer.
  • Through-rust: Holes or paper-thin metal that flexes when pressed. That panel needs patching or replacement.
Based on your photos, you're mostly dealing with surface rust — very manageable.
!
Check the interior ceiling for water stains while you're at it
Inside · 10 min
Before touching the roof from outside, look at the rafters and ceiling from inside after a rain — active drips or fresh dark staining tells you exactly where the leaks are. Old brown staining may be historical. New dark wet patches are current problems. Mark the indoor locations, then find the corresponding spots on the roof outside.

Wall assessment

DO
Check for rot at ground level and foundation contact points
Walls · 20 min
The bottom 2–3 feet of exterior barn board takes the most abuse — splash-back from rain, ground moisture wicking up, snow sitting against it in winter. Probe with a screwdriver along the base of every wall. Pay extra attention where the wood meets the stone foundation.
  • Soft or spongy = rot, needs replacement before staining
  • Gray and weathered but firm = fine, stain will revive it
  • Cracked and checking (surface splits along the grain) = normal weathering, stain will stabilize it
DO
Look for gaps, missing boards, and open knot holes
Walls · 20 min
Walk every wall and note anything that goes all the way through to the interior. These are water and critter entry points that need to be caulked or patched before staining — stain won't seal a gap. A daylight inspection from inside (doors closed, lights off) makes through-gaps obvious.
TIP
Note which walls get the most sun and which face the weather
Walls · 5 min
The wall that faces southwest gets the most UV and will need the most stain coverage and the most frequent reapplication. The wall facing the prevailing weather direction (usually west or southwest in Kentucky) will show the most moisture wear. These walls may need two coats while the others need one.
2

Corrugated metal roof — clean, treat rust, seal

Good news: surface rust on an intact corrugated metal roof is a weekend job. You're not replacing anything — you're stopping the rust where it is and putting a protective coat over everything.

How bad is your rust?

Pick the description that best matches what you see — the treatment approach changes based on severity.

🟠
Surface rust
Orange staining, metal solid underneath, no pitting
🔴
Moderate rust
Visible pitting, flaky in spots, metal still intact
⛔
Through-rust
Holes or paper-thin panels that flex under pressure

Step-by-step roof process

DO
Clear debris from valleys and around fasteners
~1 hr
Leaves, moss, and debris trapped in the corrugated valleys hold moisture against the metal and accelerate rust. Clear by hand or with a stiff brush — don't pressure wash yet, you'll push debris into gaps. Pay special attention around every fastener; debris piled around screw heads is where rust concentrates.
DO
Re-secure loose fasteners and replace missing ones
~1–2 hrs
Any screw or nail that's lifted, stripped, or missing needs to be dealt with before applying any coating — otherwise you're sealing over a leak point. Replace old nails with hex-head self-tapping metal roofing screws with neoprene washers. The washer creates a watertight seal around the fastener hole.
  • Use 1.5" or 2" self-tapping hex-head screws rated for metal roofing
  • If an old nail hole is stripped out, move 1–2 inches and put the new screw in fresh metal
  • Don't overtighten — the neoprene washer should compress slightly, not flatten completely
Hex-head metal roofing screws Neoprene washer screws
DO
Wire brush all rust spots, then apply rust converter
~2–3 hrs
Wire brush first to knock off loose flaky rust and expose the solid metal underneath. Then apply a rust converter — this is a chemical that reacts with iron oxide and turns it into a stable black compound (iron tannate) that actually bonds to the metal and stops rust from spreading.
  • Apply with a brush directly to all rusted areas
  • It will turn black as it reacts — that's correct
  • Allow full cure: 24 hrs minimum before priming or painting over
  • No need to remove all rust — the converter handles what's left after brushing
Corroseal Rust Converter Rust-Oleum Rust Reformer Permatex Rust Treatment
DO
Seal laps, ridge cap, and any gaps with butyl tape or roof caulk
~1–2 hrs
The ridge cap (peak), panel overlaps, and any penetrations (if you ever add a stovepipe or mini-split line) are where water gets in. Before applying any coating, seal these with butyl tape or a metal roof sealant.
  • Butyl tape is best for panel laps — press firmly into the overlap before screwing down
  • Geocel or Sika roof sealant for the ridge cap and any existing gaps
  • Apply sealant in temperatures above 40°F for proper adhesion
  • Smooth with a wet finger — don't leave raised ridges that will catch debris
Butyl tape (3" wide) Geocel 2300 metal roof sealant Sika Sikaflex roof sealant
DO
Apply metal roof coating — full surface
~3–4 hrs + dry time
A quality elastomeric or acrylic metal roof coating goes over everything — it's not a paint, it's a flexible waterproof membrane that expands and contracts with the metal through temperature cycles without cracking.
  • Apply with a 9" roller on a pole — get into the valleys with a brush
  • Work ridge to eave, overlapping each pass by 4–6 inches
  • One coat is a minimum; two coats is strongly recommended for a roof with any existing rust history
  • Gray or aluminum-colored coatings reflect heat — worthwhile in Kentucky summers
  • Coverage: roughly 100 sq ft per gallon for one coat
Henry 887 ElastoBarrier Rust-Oleum LeakSeal Metal Roof Gaco Roof Coating
!
Never coat a wet or damp roof
Moisture trapped under the coating causes it to bubble, lift, and peel within months. The roof must be bone dry — wait at least 48 hours after any rain, and don't apply in the morning when dew is still present. If you're unsure, run your hand across the metal — if it feels even slightly cool and damp, wait.
3

Exterior walls — clean, repair, prep

Old barn board exterior is thirsty and weathered — it'll accept stain beautifully once it's clean. The prep work here is more aggressive than the interior because you're dealing with decades of outdoor exposure.

!
Replace rotted boards before doing anything else
Varies
Any boards that failed the screwdriver test during assessment get replaced first. Staining over rotted wood seals in moisture and accelerates decay. Match the new boards in thickness — use rough-sawn lumber from a sawmill if possible, which will weather closer to the existing boards than smooth-milled lumber.

New wood will look noticeably brighter than aged barn board even after staining. Accept this — it'll weather in over 1–2 seasons to blend more naturally.
DO
Caulk all gaps and through-holes
~2 hrs
Any gap that goes through to the interior gets filled before cleaning. Use an exterior-rated paintable caulk or backer rod + caulk for larger gaps. This step also blocks water from getting behind the siding during pressure washing.
  • Gaps under ¼ inch: exterior caulk, tool smooth with a wet finger
  • Gaps ¼–¾ inch: backer rod first, then caulk over top
  • Gaps over ¾ inch: replace the board or use a thin wood filler strip
DAP Alex Flex exterior caulk Backer rod (foam rope)
DO
Pressure wash the walls — low pressure, wide fan
~2–3 hrs
The exterior can handle a pressure washer where the interior couldn't. But old barn board is soft — use low pressure (1200–1500 PSI max, 25–40 degree fan tip) and keep the wand moving. High pressure will raise the grain, fuzz the wood surface, and drive water deep into the boards.
  • Keep nozzle 12–18 inches from the surface
  • Spray with the grain, never across it
  • Don't linger on one spot
  • Start at the top and work down — let dirty water run off below your clean section
  • Allow 48–72 hrs of dry time before any stain or sealer — this is non-negotiable
25° or 40° fan tip only Max 1500 PSI on old wood
DO
Apply wood brightener after washing (optional but recommended)
~1 hr + 30 min dry
Pressure washing alone cleans the surface but can leave it looking gray-flat and can raise the wood pH (especially after a TSP wash). A wood brightener — an oxalic acid solution — neutralizes the pH, brightens the wood tone, and opens the grain for better stain penetration. Apply with a garden sprayer or brush, let dwell 15–20 min, rinse thoroughly.

This step makes a noticeable difference in how evenly the stain absorbs. Highly recommended if you want consistent color. Defy Wood Brightener Olympic Wood Cleaner & Brightener
TIP
Protect the stone foundation and vegetation before washing
Cover plants within 3 feet of the walls with plastic sheeting before washing and staining — deck cleaner and wood brightener will damage plants and grass. Wet the stone foundation before washing — saturated stone absorbs less of any cleaning solution that runs down. Rinse plants thoroughly after you're done even if covered.
4

Exterior stain & sealer — one product, two coats

Unlike the interior where stain and sealer are separate steps, the best exterior products for old barn board are penetrating semi-transparent stains that stain and seal in one application. Simpler and more durable.

Semi-transparent, not solid. A solid stain hides the wood grain completely and starts to peel as the wood moves. A semi-transparent penetrating stain soaks into the wood, shows the grain and texture, and wears away gradually rather than peeling. For old barn board, semi-transparent is always the right choice.

Pick your exterior color direction

These are the main paths for weathered barn board. The exterior color doesn't have to match the interior — in fact, a slight contrast between outside and inside can look very intentional.

Natural / cedar
Warm brown, classic barn look
Driftwood gray
Lean into the weathered tone
Dark brown / bark
Rich, dramatic, sharp contrast
Clear sealer only
No color change, protective only

Application

DO
Apply with a brush, not a sprayer — for old rough wood
~4–6 hrs per side
Spraying is faster but misses the crevices, checks, and rough texture of old barn board. A 4–5 inch natural bristle brush forces stain into every crack and groove. Work top to bottom, one board at a time, full board length without stopping.
  • Don't apply in direct sun — the stain dries too fast and streaks
  • Ideal conditions: overcast, 55–80°F, low humidity
  • Keep a wet edge — don't let a section dry before connecting to the next
  • Back-brush any drips or runs immediately
  • Allow 24–48 hrs between coats
DO
Two coats on the worst-weathered walls, one on the others
Day 2
The south and west-facing walls (most UV exposure, most weather) will absorb the first coat almost entirely. They need a second coat. The north and east walls may look fully saturated after one coat — feel for dry or lighter-colored areas; if the wood absorbed everything, it needs more.

Test by pressing your fingertip to the surface — if stain transfers to your finger, it's still wet. Bone dry = ready for second coat.
TIP
Coordinate exterior and interior stain tones deliberately
You don't need to match exactly — but a relationship between the two looks intentional. If you went dark walnut on the interior floor, a natural cedar tone on the exterior creates a warm gradient from outside in. If you went driftwood gray interior, matching driftwood exterior feels cohesive. The jarring combination to avoid: very dark exterior with very light interior — it makes the space feel like a cave the moment you step in.

Exterior timeline — weather dependent

The exterior needs a solid 4–5 day dry weather window for the final stages. Plan around the forecast. In Kentucky, late May, September, and early October are ideal windows.

Weekend 1 — any weather
Full exterior assessment
Walk the roof, probe the walls, mark all problem areas. Inside inspection for leak staining. Takes most of a Saturday.
Weekend 1 — Sunday
Supplies run + board replacement
Buy everything. Replace any rotted boards while the weather window doesn't matter yet. Caulk gaps in the walls.
Dry Weekend 2 — Saturday (watch forecast)
Roof work: debris clear + fasteners + rust treatment
Full day on the roof. Clear all valleys, re-secure fasteners, wire brush rust spots, apply rust converter. Let converter cure overnight — do not cover.
Dry Weekend 2 — Sunday
Roof: seal laps + ridge cap + coat 1
Butyl tape and sealant on all joints, then elastomeric coating over the entire surface. Roof must stay dry for 24 hrs after.
Weekday gap — 24–48 hrs
Roof coat 1 curing
No rain. Meanwhile pressure wash the walls (this step is fine in between dry days since walls need 48–72 hrs to dry before staining anyway).
Dry Weekend 3 — Saturday
Roof coat 2 + wood brightener on walls
Second coat of roof coating in the morning. Apply wood brightener to walls in the afternoon. Rinse brightener after 20 min dwell time.
Dry Weekend 3 — Sunday + 2 weekday evenings
Wall stain — all four sides
Start with the worst-weathered wall (usually south or west). Weekday evenings can knock out a wall each if you have 2–3 hrs of daylight. Two coats on south/west, one on north/east.
48 hrs after final coat
Done — exterior protected
Full cure in 5–7 days. Plan to re-stain the south and west walls in 2–3 years; north and east should hold 4+ years.
Total elapsed time: 3 weekends over roughly 4–5 weeks, weather permitting. The roof can be done independently of the walls if you get a good 2-day window early. Don't try to compress the roof and wall work into the same weekend — you'll be rushed on the roof and the walls won't have dried properly.
