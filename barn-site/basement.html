The Stone Basement

13 × 13 ft · Limestone walls · Brick floor · New door · Fixed windows · Bulk storage

✦

What you're working with — and what it becomes

A 13×13 ft dry-stacked limestone basement with a brick floor and ~7 ft ceiling. Currently an open junk space with no door and three unglazed window openings. The goal: clean, weathertight, dry storage for bikes, seasonal bins, and lawn equipment — at very low cost.

What's there now

  • Dry-stacked limestone walls — solid and beautiful
  • Brick floor — fairly flat, appears intact
  • ~7 ft ceiling height — full standing clearance
  • Three window openings — unglazed or mesh-covered
  • One doorway opening — frame present, no door
  • Existing timber shelf structure / loft framing above
  • Active junk: hoses, garden tools, old lumber
  • Unknown moisture history — needs rain check

What it becomes

  • Weathertight exterior door — locking
  • Three fixed glazed windows — light + ventilation
  • Sealed brick floor — swept clean, stabilized
  • Dehumidifier on a drain or bucket
  • Bike hooks on wall — all bikes off the floor
  • Wall shelving for labeled seasonal totes
  • Lawn equipment zone — mower, hoses, tools
  • Stone walls left raw — they're already stunning
The stone walls don't need anything done to them. Dry-stacked limestone like this is self-draining by design — water moves through the gaps rather than pooling behind the wall. Don't seal, paint, or parge the interior stone face. Leave it raw. It will look incredible once the space is clean and lit.
Cost perspective: This is the lowest cost-per-square-foot addition in the whole project. You're getting 169 sq ft of covered, lockable storage for roughly $800–1,800 in materials. The limestone walls and brick floor are already there — you're just making them usable.

1

Assessment — before touching anything

The basement has some specific things to check that differ from the upper barn. Stone foundations and brick floors have their own failure modes. Spend a focused hour down there with good lighting before buying a single thing.

DO
Check the stone wall mortar — or lack of it
Walls · 20 min
Dry-stacked limestone (no mortar) is what you appear to have — this is actually the traditional and preferred construction for this region. The stones hold each other by weight and friction. What to check:
  • Are any stones visibly shifted, tilted, or leaning inward? These need to be reset before loading the space with heavy storage
  • Any large gaps (2"+ wide) that go all the way through to exterior? These are critter entry points — stuff with hardware cloth before closing up
  • Are the top courses (where the wall meets the wood structure above) solid? This is the most common failure point
If any stones seem genuinely unstable, this is a job for a stonemason before you proceed. Most old Kentucky limestone foundations are remarkably solid — don't be alarmed by surface weathering.
DO
Inspect the brick floor — look for heaving or soft spots
Floor · 15 min
Walk every square foot. What you're looking for:
  • Heaved bricks — ones that have pushed up higher than neighbors, usually from frost or tree roots. These get knocked flat and re-set before sealing
  • Sunken areas — depressions where the subbase has settled. Fill with sand-set mortar before sealing
  • Loose or missing bricks — mark them, they need to be reset or replaced
  • White efflorescence (chalky mineral deposits) — normal and harmless, clean off with diluted white vinegar before sealing
DO
Measure all three window openings precisely
Windows · 20 min
Measure each opening in at least three places — width at top, middle, and bottom; height on left, center, and right. Stone openings are almost never perfectly square. The smallest measurement is your rough opening size — your window unit needs to fit within that. Write each one down separately; they're likely all different sizes.

Also note the wall thickness at each opening — typically 12–18" for limestone foundations. This determines what type of window frame and sill you need. Tape measure Notepad — measure 3× per opening
DO
Measure the door opening — height, width, and squareness
Door · 15 min
Same approach as windows — measure at multiple points. Also check:
  • Is the existing door frame/lintel still solid? The lintel (the stone or timber spanning the top of the opening) carries the wall weight above — if it's cracked or sagging it needs attention before hanging a door
  • Is the threshold (bottom of the opening) at grade, above grade, or below? This affects drainage and whether you need a threshold sill
  • Which direction does the door need to swing? From the photos it appears to open outward — confirm this is what you want
!
Check for active critter habitation before spending time down there
Safety · 5 min
Open spaces like this with multiple unglazed openings are prime habitat for bats, snakes, and wasps. Do a quick sweep with a flashlight before spending extended time in the space. Bats in Kentucky are protected — if you find a colony, contact your county extension office before disturbing them. Wasps can be handled with standard wasp spray at dusk.
TIP
Do your moisture check now — visit after a significant rain
The best assessment happens 24 hours after a heavy rain. Walk the floor looking for standing water, wet patches, or water staining on the walls. Dry-stacked stone allows water to weep through and drain — some seeping during heavy rain is normal and not a dealbreaker. Standing water that doesn't drain within a few hours is a concern requiring grading work outside before you store anything down here.
2

Moisture control — the make-or-break step

A limestone basement that stays dry is a fantastic storage space. One that stays wet is a mold factory. Address moisture before anything else goes in — no exceptions.

Understand what you're dealing with first

DO
Identify the moisture source — there are three types
Type 1 — Condensation: Warm humid air hits the cool stone walls and condenses. Looks like sweating walls, especially in summer. Fix: ventilation + dehumidifier. Very common and easy to manage.

Type 2 — Seepage through walls: Water migrates through the stone and mortar during rain. Creates wet patches or weeping on wall face. Fix: exterior grading to direct water away from foundation. Do NOT seal the interior wall — you'll trap water inside the stone.

Type 3 — Floor infiltration: Water comes up through or around the brick floor. Usually from a high water table or poor drainage. Fix: this is the most serious type and may require a French drain or sump — get a waterproofing contractor's opinion if you see this.
DO
Check and improve exterior grading around the foundation
Outside · 1–2 hrs
The ground around the basement should slope away from the foundation at least 6 inches over 10 feet. From the exterior photos there's significant vegetation, debris, and ivy growing against the stone — this traps moisture and accelerates wall damage. Clear it all back 2–3 feet from the foundation, then assess the grade.
  • Remove ivy, brush, and debris from against the foundation — this is exterior work that should happen anyway
  • If the ground slopes toward the building, add topsoil to create positive drainage away
  • Make sure downspouts discharge at least 6 ft from the foundation
DO
Install a dehumidifier with continuous drain
Year-round · essential
Even a dry basement will see elevated humidity in summer. A 30–50 pint dehumidifier running continuously is the single most effective thing you can do for long-term storage protection. Set it to maintain 50–55% relative humidity.
  • Place it in the center of the space for best air circulation
  • Connect to a drain hose that runs to a floor drain or exits through a window opening — you don't want to be emptying a bucket in a basement storage space
  • The drain hose can run out through one of the window openings before you glaze them — plan the routing before installing windows
  • Run it May through October at minimum; year-round is better
Frigidaire 50-pt dehumidifier — $180–250 20 ft drain hose — $15
!
Never store fabric, paper, or electronics here without confirmed dryness
Plastic totes with sealed lids protect most seasonal items fine. But anything fabric (camping gear, tarps, blankets), cardboard boxes, or electronics needs the dehumidifier running and humidity confirmed below 60% before storing. If in doubt, store those categories upstairs and put only hard plastic, metal, and rubber items in the basement.
3

Clean out — fully, before any other work

Everything out. The existing timber shelf structure, all the tools, hoses, lumber, leaves — all of it. You cannot assess the floor, walls, or openings properly with junk in the way. This is also when you discover what surprises are hiding in the corners.

DO
Remove everything including the existing metal shelving
~3–4 hrs
The metal shelving unit visible in the photos can come back in if it's in good shape — but get it out first so you can work freely and assess the wall behind it. Check it for rust damage while it's out; if the shelves are solid, clean, paint with Rust-Oleum, and re-use.
  • Sort as you go: trash / keep / move to upper barn temporarily
  • The hoses, garden tools, and lumber scraps are likely all staying in some form — just staging them outside temporarily
  • Old lumber: assess each piece. Straight, sound boards can be reclaimed for shelving. Rotted or warped ones go
DO
Sweep and blow out all debris — leaves, dirt, cobwebs
~1 hr
A leaf blower through the open door opening is the fastest way to clear the bulk. Follow with a stiff push broom. Get into every corner and along the base of the walls where debris accumulates. Once swept, you'll be able to see the brick floor properly and spot any problem areas.
DO
Stuff all wall gaps with hardware cloth — temporary critter block
~30 min
Before you walk away from a clean empty space, stuff any gaps larger than ½" in the stone walls with crumpled hardware cloth (metal mesh). This prevents re-infestation while you work on the door and windows over the following weeks. It's temporary — you'll mortar or properly seal once the space is finished. ¼" hardware cloth — $15–20 for a roll
TIP
The existing loft/shelf framing above — decide now
The timber framing visible in the photos (the loft-like structure spanning the space) — decide whether it stays or goes before doing anything else. If it's solid, it could become very useful overhead storage shelving with minimal work. If it's rotted, sagging, or just in the way, remove it now while the space is empty. Don't work around it for months and then decide to pull it.
4

Installing the new door — the most important weathertighting step

A stone opening without a square frame is the challenge here. You're not hanging a door in drywall — you need to build a proper wooden door frame within the stone opening first, then hang the door in that frame. This is a half-day job that makes an enormous difference.

Choosing the right door

DO
Get a pre-hung exterior door — not a slab door
A pre-hung door comes with the door already mounted in its own frame with hinges attached. You install the whole unit — frame and door together — into the rough opening. This is far simpler than building a frame and hanging a slab separately, and produces a better weathertight result.
  • Measure your rough opening and buy the closest standard size that fits within it (standard: 32"×80" or 36"×80")
  • You'll shim the pre-hung unit within the rough opening to make it level and plumb
  • Go for a solid wood or fiberglass exterior door — steel is fine too but may look out of character against the stone
  • A simple plank-style wood door (board-and-batten) would look fantastic and authentic against the limestone
Pre-hung exterior door 32"×80" — $200–500 Board-and-batten style recommended

Installation — step by step

DO
Build a rough buck frame inside the stone opening
~2 hrs
A "buck" is a rectangular wood frame built to fill the stone opening and give you a square, plumb surface to attach the pre-hung door to. Without it, you're trying to attach a door to irregular stone — which won't work.
  • Cut 2×6 or 2×8 lumber to form a rectangle that fits snugly within the stone opening
  • The width of the lumber should roughly match the wall thickness so the frame sits flush inside
  • Use a level religiously — even small deviations here cause the door to swing open or closed on its own
  • Anchor the buck to the stone using masonry anchors or Tapcon screws driven into the stone
  • Fill any gaps between the buck and stone with expanding foam insulation — this weatherseals and locks the frame in place
2×6 or 2×8 lumber Tapcon masonry screws + hammer drill Great Stuff expanding foam
DO
Set the pre-hung unit into the buck — shim, level, fasten
~2 hrs
This is the same process as hanging a door in a house — just into your buck frame instead of standard stud framing.
  • Test fit the pre-hung unit first before fastening anything
  • Shim from the hinge side first — hinge side must be perfectly plumb
  • Check that the door opens and closes freely before driving any screws
  • Fasten through the pre-hung frame into the buck with 3" screws — hit both sides and top
  • Check and recheck level and plumb after every fastener — things shift
  • Test swing again: door should stay where you leave it (not swing open or closed)
DO
Seal and trim — inside and outside
~1 hr
  • Fill any remaining gap between the pre-hung frame and the buck with expanding foam — let cure, then trim flush with a utility knife
  • Outside: apply flashing tape over the top of the door frame where it meets the stone, to direct water away
  • Outside: caulk the joint between the door frame and stone with a flexible exterior caulk (not rigid — stone moves seasonally)
  • Inside: simple wood casing trim covers the gap between frame and stone face — nail into the buck frame, not the stone
  • Install a threshold with a door sweep on the bottom of the door to seal the gap at the sill
Flashing tape Exterior caulk (flexible) Door sweep / threshold
TIP
Add a deadbolt — this space will have bikes and equipment in it
A basic keyed deadbolt takes 20 minutes to install and makes the basement secure. Use the same key as the barn's main doors if possible so you're not carrying multiple keys. Schlage or Kwikset both make weatherized exterior deadbolts for around $30–50. Schlage B60N exterior deadbolt — $35–55
!
Check the lintel above the door opening before hanging anything
The lintel is the stone or timber that spans the top of the door opening and carries the wall load above it. Before installing the buck or hanging the door, inspect it carefully. If it's a large flat stone: look for cracks running vertically from its ends — these indicate the stone is splitting under load and needs to be addressed by a stonemason before you hang a door. If it's a timber lintel: probe for rot with a screwdriver.
5

Fixing the three windows — same buck principle, simpler execution

The window openings get the same treatment as the door: a wood buck frame built into the stone opening, then a window unit set into it. For a basement storage space, fixed (non-opening) windows are the right call — simpler, cheaper, and more secure.

What type of window to use

DO
Use fixed glass block or fixed single-pane windows — not casements
For a storage basement, you have three good options:

Option 1 — Glass block panels: Pre-assembled glass block panels fit directly into the opening with mortar or a vinyl frame kit. No buck needed for standard sizes. Extremely durable, very secure (can't be broken easily), great light transmission, zero maintenance. Best option if the openings are roughly square. Cost: $40–80 per panel.

Option 2 — Fixed single-pane window with buck: A simple fixed-pane window unit (no sash, just glass in a frame) set into a buck. Gives more light than glass block and looks more like a traditional window. Cost: $60–150 per unit for custom or standard sizes.

Option 3 — Polycarbonate panel: Corrugated or flat polycarbonate (like Twinwall) cut to fit and screwed to a buck frame. Cheapest option, lets light through, very easy to install. Not as attractive but completely functional. Cost: $20–40 per opening.

Recommendation for your space: Glass block for the two smaller openings, fixed single-pane for the largest one if you want a more finished look. Or all glass block for simplicity and maximum security. Pittsburgh Corning glass block — $40–80/panel Vinyl frame glass block kit — easiest install
DO
Build a buck frame for each window opening
~1 hr per window
Same principle as the door buck but smaller. Use 2×6 lumber (or match your wall thickness) to build a rectangular frame that fits within the stone opening.
  • Cut four pieces: two for sides (full height of opening), one for top, one for bottom sill
  • Assemble as a rectangle, checking for square with a diagonal measurement
  • Slide into the opening and shim until level and plumb
  • Anchor to stone with Tapcon masonry screws — 2 per side minimum
  • Foam the gaps between buck and stone — both inside and outside
  • The window unit then installs into the buck exactly as it would in a house wall
DO
Installing glass block panels — mortar or vinyl frame method
~1.5 hrs per window
Vinyl frame kit method (easiest): Glass block kits come with a vinyl channel frame that you install into the buck opening first, then snap the glass blocks into. No mortar, no mess.
  • Cut the vinyl channels to fit your buck opening
  • Snap channels onto the perimeter of the glass block assembly
  • Set the whole assembly into the buck opening
  • Caulk the joint between vinyl frame and buck with silicone
  • Done — no mortar cure time needed
Mortar method (more traditional, stronger):
  • Mix glass block mortar (white, pre-mixed)
  • Lay blocks one row at a time with ⅜" mortar joints
  • Use plastic spacers to keep joints consistent
  • Allow 24 hrs to cure before any stress on the panel
  • Tool the joints smooth and clean off mortar haze with a damp sponge
Glass block mortar — $12–18 Glass block spacers — $8 Vinyl glass block frame kit
DO
Flash and caulk the exterior of each window
~20 min per window
Water running down the stone face will try to get in at the top of each window frame. A strip of self-adhesive flashing tape over the top joint directs water over and past the frame.
  • Apply flashing tape to the top of the window buck where it meets the stone
  • Lap the tape at least 4" onto the stone face above and 4" onto the window frame below
  • Then caulk all four sides of the window-to-stone joint with flexible exterior caulk
  • On the sill (bottom), create a slight outward slope so water drains away from the building — a sloped wood sill board or mortar slope does this
TIP
Route your dehumidifier drain through one window before glazing
Before you install the last window, run the dehumidifier drain hose through the opening and cap around it. Once all three windows are in, you'd have to drill through stone or glass block to add a drain exit — much harder. Plan the drain route first, then install windows around it.
6

The brick floor — clean, reset, seal

The brick floor is a genuine feature — old handmade brick is beautiful and durable. The goal is to stabilize what's there, level any problem spots, and seal it so it's cleanable and dust-free. You are not replacing it.

DO
Reset any heaved, sunken, or loose bricks
~2–3 hrs
Old brick floors are sand-set — the bricks sit on a compacted sand bed with no mortar between them (or very old, crumbled mortar). This makes resetting easy:
  • Pry up the problem brick with a flat bar — it should come up easily
  • Add or remove sand underneath to bring it to the right height
  • Re-set the brick, check it's level with neighbors
  • Sweep dry sand or dry-set mortar into the joints around it
  • For missing bricks: antique brick salvage yards often have matching old brick — worth the trip for authenticity
Flat pry bar Coarse sand or polymeric sand Rubber mallet for setting
DO
Clean the brick — TSP wash, then rinse
~1.5 hrs + dry time
Same TSP wash used on the interior barn walls works perfectly on brick. Mix at label ratio, scrub with a stiff brush, let dwell 10 minutes, rinse thoroughly.
  • For efflorescence (white chalky deposits): scrub with diluted white vinegar (1:1 with water) first, let sit 10 min, rinse. Then do the TSP wash.
  • For oil or fuel stains from old equipment: apply cat litter or baking soda to absorb, leave overnight, sweep up, then TSP wash
  • Allow 48 hrs to dry completely before sealing — brick is very absorbent
DO
Seal with a penetrating masonry sealer — matte finish
~1.5 hrs + cure time
A penetrating masonry sealer soaks into the brick and mortar, binds the surface so it doesn't dust, repels moisture, and makes it cleanable — without changing the appearance or making it slippery.
  • Do NOT use a film-forming sealer or epoxy coating — it will peel as moisture migrates through the brick
  • Apply with a pump garden sprayer — saturate the surface, then back-brush to work it in
  • One coat is usually sufficient; a second coat on very absorbent brick is worthwhile
  • Allow 24 hrs before foot traffic, 72 hrs before placing heavy storage
Surecrete Eco-Finish (penetrating) — $45–65/gal Foundation Armor SX5000 — $60–80/gal Pump garden sprayer
TIP
The brick floor will look dramatically better after cleaning alone
Old brick that's been covered in debris and grime for years often reveals beautiful color variation and patina once cleaned. Don't be surprised if the floor looks completely different after the TSP wash — in a very good way. Take before and after photos; it's one of the most satisfying transformations in this whole project.
7

Storage setup — what goes where

With the door and windows in and the floor sealed, you're ready to set up storage. The layout is simple: bikes on the wall opposite the door, seasonal bins along the back wall, lawn equipment in the center floor zone near the door for easy access.

Zone layout

DO
Bike hooks — mount into the stone with masonry anchors
Right wall or back wall
You can absolutely mount bike hooks directly into the limestone wall using masonry anchors. This is more secure than you might expect — dry-stacked stone is very hard.
  • Use a hammer drill with a masonry bit to drill into the stone (not the gaps between stones)
  • Install sleeve anchors or Tapcon screws rated for the hook load
  • Alternatively: mount a 2×8 horizontal board to the wall first (anchored at multiple points), then hang all hooks from the board — gives you flexibility to reposition hooks without drilling new holes in the stone
  • Aim for wall space that doesn't block the window light or the door swing
Hammer drill + ½" masonry bit Sleeve anchors or Tapcon 3" Bike wall hooks — $12–18 each
DO
Seasonal bin shelving — freestanding, not wall-mounted
Back wall or side wall
Given the stone walls, freestanding heavy-duty shelving is easier than wall-mounting for heavy bin storage. A metal utility shelf (the kind used in garages) requires no anchoring and holds enormous weight.
  • Husky or Edsal metal shelving — 4–5 shelf units, 48"×18"×72" — $60–100 each
  • Position against the back wall or side wall away from the door
  • Same labeled bin system as planned upstairs — Sterilite 27-gal totes for seasonal items
  • Sleds and long items hang on wall hooks above the shelving
Husky 5-shelf steel unit — $65–90
DO
Lawn equipment zone — center floor, near the door
Mower, pressure washer, hose reels, garden tools — anything you roll in and out goes in the floor zone nearest the door. The goal is one clear path: open door, roll equipment out, done.
  • Wall-mount a tool rack for long-handled tools (rakes, shovels, brooms) — keeps them vertical and out of the walkway
  • Hose reels mount to the wall or sit on a small shelf near the door
  • Keep this zone intentionally limited — it's the "active" zone, not overflow storage
Wall-mount tool rack — $25–40 Hose reel wall mount — $20–35
TIP
Add one light — battery-powered LED is enough to start
Before you have proper electrical run to the basement, a battery-powered motion-sensor LED light does the job surprisingly well. Mount one near the door and one at the back wall. When you eventually run electrical (from the upper barn), you can wire a proper switched fixture — the stone walls and exposed beam ceiling would look stunning with a couple of vintage-style cage bulb fixtures. Govee battery LED motion light — $20–30 Later: run conduit from upper barn electrical
