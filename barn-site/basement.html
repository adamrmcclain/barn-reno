1

Assessment — before touching anything

The basement has specific things to check that differ from the upper barn. Stone foundations and brick floors have their own failure modes. Spend a focused hour down there with good lighting before buying a single thing.

DO
Check the stone wall condition
Walls · 20 min
Dry-stacked limestone holds itself by weight and friction — no mortar needed. What to check:
  • Any stones visibly shifted, tilted, or leaning inward? These need resetting before loading with heavy storage
  • Large gaps (2"+ wide) going all the way through? These are critter entry points — stuff temporarily with hardware cloth
  • Top courses where wall meets the wood structure above — most common failure point, check carefully
Most old Kentucky limestone foundations are remarkably solid. Surface weathering and moss don't indicate structural problems.
DO
Inspect the lintel above the door opening
Critical · 10 min
The photo shows a timber lintel spanning the top of the door opening. This carries the stone load above — it must be sound before you hang any door.
  • Probe with a screwdriver along its length — it should feel solid, not spongy
  • Look for sagging (anything more than ¼" across the span is worth watching)
  • If it's rotted or cracked through, it needs replacing before the door goes in — a stonemason or structural carpenter can advise
  • From the photo it looks intact — but confirm in person with a close inspection
DO
Re-measure the opening carefully — in multiple places
Door · 20 min
You have 78"×41" as your measurement — good. Now take three more measurements:
  • Width: measure at the top, middle, and bottom of the opening. Stone openings are almost never perfectly parallel — note the smallest measurement, that's your constraint
  • Height: measure on the left, center, and right. Note any difference — an uneven sill affects your threshold approach
  • Wall thickness at the opening: measure from the face of the stone on the outside to the inside face — typically 12–20" in a limestone foundation. This determines your buck frame depth
  • Square check: measure both diagonals of the opening — if they're within ½" of each other it's close enough to square; if not, note which corners are out
Write all of this down. Your door will be built to the final confirmed dimensions.
DO
Measure all three window openings
Windows · 15 min
Same approach — three measurements per dimension, note the smallest. Each opening is likely a different size. Write them down separately; they'll each need their own buck frame and glass panel.
!
Check for active critter habitation
Safety · 5 min first
Open spaces like this with unglazed openings are prime habitat for bats, wasps, and snakes in Kentucky. Do a quick sweep with a flashlight before spending extended time in the space. Bats are protected — if you find a colony, contact your county extension office. Wasps: standard spray at dusk. Snakes: most Kentucky species are non-venomous and will leave on their own once the space is cleaned out and sealed.
TIP
Visit after heavy rain for the moisture check
The best assessment of moisture happens 24 hours after a significant rain. Look for standing water, wet patches on the floor, or weeping through the stone face. Some seeping through dry-stacked stone during heavy rain is normal and not a dealbreaker — standing water that doesn't drain within a few hours is more concerning.
2

Moisture control — the make-or-break step

A limestone basement that stays dry is a fantastic storage space. One that stays wet is a mold factory. Address moisture before anything else goes in — no exceptions.

DO
Identify the moisture source — three types, three fixes
Type 1 — Condensation: Warm humid air hits cool stone walls and condenses. Looks like sweating walls, especially in summer. Fix: ventilation + dehumidifier. Very common, easy to manage.

Type 2 — Seepage through walls: Water migrates through the stone during heavy rain. Creates wet patches on the wall face. Fix: exterior grading to direct water away. Do NOT seal the interior wall face — you'll trap water inside the stone and accelerate damage.

Type 3 — Floor infiltration: Water comes up through or around the brick floor — high water table or poor sub-drainage. Fix: this is the most serious type and may require a French drain or sump pump. Get a waterproofing contractor's opinion if you see this consistently.
DO
Clear vegetation from the exterior foundation — it's trapping moisture
Outside · 1–2 hrs
The exterior photos show significant ivy, brush, and debris against the stone — this is actively holding moisture against the wall. Clear everything back 2–3 feet from the foundation before doing anything inside.
  • Pull ivy from the stone face — don't cut it, pull the roots out or they regrow
  • Remove all debris piled against the stone
  • Make sure the ground slopes away from the foundation — add topsoil if needed
  • Ensure downspouts discharge at least 6 ft away from the building
DO
Install a dehumidifier with continuous drain — run it year-round
Essential
Even a dry limestone basement sees elevated humidity in summer. A 30–50 pint dehumidifier on continuous drain is the single most effective thing you can do for long-term storage protection.
  • Set to maintain 50–55% relative humidity
  • Route drain hose out through one window opening before glazing — plan this before installing windows
  • Place in the center of the space for best air circulation
  • Run May through October at minimum; year-round is better
Frigidaire 50-pt dehumidifier — $180–250 20 ft drain hose — $15
!
Don't seal the stone walls — ever
This bears repeating because it's counterintuitive. Sealing, painting, or parging (applying mortar render) the interior face of dry-stacked limestone walls traps moisture inside the stone. When it freezes and thaws, it spalls the stone face off. The walls breathe by design — let them.
3

Clean out — fully, before any other work

Everything out of the basement before touching the door, windows, or floor. You cannot assess or work around a cluttered space. This is also when you discover what surprises are in the corners.

DO
Remove everything — including the existing metal shelving
~3–4 hrs
The metal shelving unit can come back in if it's solid — pull it out now, inspect for rust, clean and repaint with Rust-Oleum if worth keeping. Sort everything as you go: trash, keep, stage outside temporarily.
  • Old lumber scraps: sort the straight sound boards from the rotted ones — usable reclaimed lumber can become shelving
  • Tools and equipment: everything stages outside or in the upper barn while you do the door, windows, and floor work
  • The timber loft framing structure visible in the earlier photos: decide now if it stays or goes — if rotted or unstable, remove it while the space is empty; if solid, it becomes useful overhead storage
DO
Sweep and blow out all debris
~1 hr
A leaf blower through the open door is the fastest way to clear the bulk — leaves, sawdust, dirt. Follow with a stiff push broom into every corner and along the stone wall base. Once swept you'll see the brick floor clearly and identify any problem areas before you start resetting bricks.
DO
Stuff all large wall gaps with hardware cloth temporarily
~30 min
Before walking away from a clean empty space, stuff any gaps larger than ½" in the stone with crumpled hardware cloth. This blocks re-infestation while you work on the door and windows over the following weeks. It's temporary — you can mortar or properly seal the gaps once the main work is done. ¼" hardware cloth — $15–20 per roll
4

The custom door — 41" × 78" board-and-batten

At 41" wide and 78" tall, this is a non-standard opening. No off-the-shelf pre-hung door fits it. The right solution is a board-and-batten door — the most historically authentic option for a limestone barn, completely DIY-buildable, and genuinely handsome. You'll build the door first, then build the buck frames around it to fit.

Door components — annotated

Step 1 — Build the two buck frames

DO
Replace both sides with new 2×6 buck boards — anchored with sleeve anchors
~2–3 hrs
Your dad is right — sleeve anchors (also called expansion anchors or expansion bolts) are the correct fastener for anchoring into limestone. They work by expanding inside a drilled hole and gripping the stone. Tapcon screws also work but sleeve anchors give more holding strength in the thick rough stone you're dealing with.

You're replacing both sides — the left frame that's there now gets pulled (it sounds like it's just wedged in anyway) and a fresh 2×6 goes in on both sides. This gives you a clean, known-square starting point.
  • Cut two 2×6 boards to the full height of the opening (78")
  • Hold each board plumb against the stone face and mark drill locations — aim for 4 anchor points per side, spaced evenly (roughly every 18")
  • Remove the board, drill into the stone with a hammer drill and the correct masonry bit for your anchor diameter (typically ½")
  • Drill at least 3" deep into solid stone — avoid drilling into gaps between stones
  • Tap the sleeve anchor into the hole, position the board over the anchor bolt, add washer and nut, tighten until snug (don't overtighten — limestone can crack)
  • Check plumb after tightening — shim behind the board if the stone face isn't flat
½" × 3.5" sleeve anchors (1 box) — $20–30 Hammer drill — rent $40/day ½" masonry bit 2×6 boards × 2 (8 ft each)
DO
Add a top buck board and check the full frame for square
~1 hr
A 2×6 across the top of the opening (just below the timber lintel) completes the buck frame and gives you a surface to attach the door stop and weatherstrip to.
  • Cut to fit snugly between the two side bucks
  • Anchor to the lintel with 3" structural screws — pre-drill to avoid splitting the timber
  • If the lintel is stone rather than timber, anchor with sleeve anchors same as the sides
  • Check both diagonals of the completed frame — they should be within ¼" of each other
  • Fill any gaps between the buck and stone with expanding foam — this locks the frame and seals air gaps
Great Stuff expanding foam (×2 cans) 3" structural screws

Step 2 — Build the board-and-batten door

DO
Cut and assemble the boards — 6× 1×6, edge to edge
~2 hrs
A 41" wide door built from 1×6 boards needs 7 boards (7 × 5.5" actual = 38.5" + gaps ≈ 41"). Leave a ⅛–¼" gap between boards to allow for seasonal expansion — a door with zero gaps will bind in humid weather.
  • Use clear or #2 cedar or pine 1×6 — cedar is more rot-resistant and worth the slight premium for an exterior door
  • Cut all boards to exactly 78" (your door height)
  • Lay them flat on sawhorses, side by side with consistent spacing
  • The battens and braces go on while it's flat — assemble the whole door on the ground, then hang it
1×6 × 8 ft cedar or pine (×7) — $8–15 each
DO
Attach three horizontal battens and a Z-brace — this is what holds it square
~1.5 hrs
The battens are 1×4 boards running horizontally across the back of the door, screwed through into each vertical board. The Z-brace runs diagonally between battens to prevent the door from racking (going out of square) over time — critical for a wide door.
  • Place battens at: top (6" from top edge), bottom (6" from bottom edge), and middle
  • Fasten each batten with 2 screws per board — 1⅝" screws, countersunk so heads sit flush
  • Do NOT use nails — they work loose over time with seasonal wood movement
  • The Z-brace runs from the bottom hinge corner diagonally up to the top latch corner — this direction is load-bearing when the door hangs
  • Cut the Z-brace ends at the angle of the diagonal (measure and mark, don't guess)
  • Screw the Z-brace to each batten where they cross
1×4 × 8 ft boards (×4) — $6–10 each 1⅝" exterior-rated screws (1 lb box)
DO
Seal all surfaces before hanging — oil or exterior primer
~1 hr + dry time
Seal the door while it's flat on sawhorses — much easier than after hanging. Pay special attention to end grain (top and bottom of each board) which absorbs water fastest.
  • Apply a penetrating exterior wood oil or solid-color exterior stain — something that won't peel
  • Match or complement the exterior barn wall stain you chose
  • End grain gets double the coats — brush it on, let absorb, brush again
  • Let cure per product directions before hanging — typically 24–48 hrs
Cabot Australian Timber Oil (exterior) — $35–50/qt Or solid exterior stain to match barn

Step 3 — Hang the door

DO
Use heavy-duty strap hinges — 3 of them
~1.5 hrs · two-person job
A 41" wide board-and-batten door is heavy — potentially 60–80 lbs. Use three heavy-duty strap hinges, not two standard hinges. Position them at top, bottom, and center.
  • Strap hinges look historically appropriate on a barn door and are rated for the weight
  • Mount the hinge barrel to the buck frame first, then hang the door into it — easier than trying to align both sides simultaneously
  • Use 2.5" screws into the buck frame — not into the stone behind it
  • The door will be heavy — have a second person hold it while you drive the screws
  • Check swing before fully tightening: door should open freely and stay where you leave it
Heavy-duty strap hinges 12" (×3) — $15–25 each Waxing the hinge barrel prevents squeaking
DO
Add weatherstrip, a door stop, and a threshold
~1 hr
Without weatherstrip the door won't seal properly — air, insects, and water will work around the edges.
  • Door stop: a 1×2 strip nailed to the inside face of the buck on the latch side — the door closes against it and stops flush
  • Weatherstrip: foam or rubber V-strip tacked to the door stop on all three sides (top and both sides). Compresses when the door closes
  • Door sweep: a rubber sweep on the door bottom seals the gap at the sill — the most important gap to close for critters and water
  • Threshold: a beveled aluminum or wood threshold set into the sill directs water away from the opening and gives the sweep something to seal against
V-strip weatherstrip — $10–15 Door sweep with rubber seal — $15–25 Aluminum threshold — $20–35
DO
Install a deadbolt — keyed exterior cylinder
~30 min
The door is thick enough (2.5"+) for a standard deadbolt. Drill a 2⅛" hole through the door face for the cylinder and a 1" hole through the edge for the bolt — a deadbolt installation kit from any hardware store comes with the hole saw sizes.
  • Position the deadbolt at a comfortable height — typically 36–42" from the bottom
  • The strike plate goes into the buck frame on the latch side
  • Use a keyed-both-sides deadbolt for a storage space (no interior thumb turn to lose)
Schlage B60N deadbolt — $35–55
TIP
Add a cane bolt or barrel bolt at the bottom for stability
A wide board-and-batten door can flex slightly in wind if only held by a deadbolt at mid-height. A simple barrel bolt or cane bolt at the bottom of the door (sliding into a hole drilled in the threshold) locks the bottom in place and eliminates flex. $10–15 and 20 minutes to install. Heavy-duty barrel bolt — $10–18
!
Exterior caulk along all buck-to-stone joints after hanging
Once the door is hung and operating correctly, go around the outside perimeter of the buck frame where it meets the stone and caulk with a flexible exterior caulk. Flashing tape over the top joint directs water off the lintel and past the frame. These two steps are what make the difference between a weathertight installation and one that leaks. Flashing tape (4" wide roll) DAP Alex Flex exterior caulk
5

Fixing the three windows — same buck principle, simpler

Same approach as the door bucks: a wood frame anchored into the stone with sleeve anchors, then a fixed window unit or glass block panel set into it. For a storage basement, fixed (non-opening) windows are the right call — simpler, more secure, no hardware to fail.

DO
Choose your glazing — glass block is the best option here
Glass block (recommended): Pre-assembled panels that install into the buck opening. Extremely durable, very secure, great light transmission, zero maintenance. The vinyl frame kit version requires no mortar — just snap into the channel and caulk. Cost: $40–80 per panel.

Fixed single-pane window: A simple fixed window unit (no operable sash) set into the buck. More light, more traditional look. Cost: $60–150 per unit for custom or close-to-standard sizes.

Polycarbonate panel: Cheapest option — cut to fit, screwed to the buck face. Lets light through, easy to replace. Less attractive. Cost: $20–40 per opening.
DO
Build a buck frame for each window — same sleeve anchor method
~1 hr per window
Same principle as the door bucks — just smaller. 2×6 lumber (match your wall thickness) forming a rectangle anchored into the stone with sleeve anchors.
  • Drill into solid stone, not into gaps between stones
  • 2 anchors per side minimum, 1 at top
  • Foam all gaps between buck and stone after setting
  • Check level and square before foam cures — you can adjust until it sets
DO
Route your dehumidifier drain through one window before glazing
Plan this first
Before installing the last window, run the dehumidifier drain hose through the opening. Drill a small hole through the buck frame, feed the hose through, seal around it with foam and silicone. Once all three windows are glazed, adding a drain exit means drilling through stone or glass block — much harder.
DO
Flash and caulk every window — outside, top joint first
~20 min per window
  • Self-adhesive flashing tape over the top of each window buck — laps 4" onto stone above and 4" onto frame below
  • Exterior caulk (flexible) around all four sides of the buck-to-stone joint
  • Create a slight outward slope at the bottom sill — wood shim or mortar — so water drains away from the building
Flashing tape (4" wide) DAP Alex Flex exterior caulk
6

The brick floor — clean, reset, seal

Old handmade brick is a genuine feature — beautiful and durable. The goal is to stabilize what's there, level any problem spots, and seal it so it's cleanable and dust-free. You are not replacing it. Note that the threshold area at the door is currently dirt/leaves — this needs a small amount of brick-setting work to bring the floor to the door sill level.

DO
Address the threshold first — dirt area at the door opening
~1–2 hrs
The photo shows the floor at the door threshold is dirt and leaves, not brick. Before doing anything else on the floor, establish the threshold:
  • Dig out the debris at the threshold to a consistent depth
  • Compact the subbase with a hand tamper
  • Add a 1–2" sand base, level it
  • Set salvaged matching brick in the sand to bring it flush with the existing brick floor inside
  • This gives you a clean transition between outside grade and inside floor — and something for your door threshold/sweep to seal against
Antique brick salvage yard for matching brick Coarse sand (1 bag) Hand tamper
DO
Reset heaved or sunken bricks
~2–3 hrs
Old brick floors are sand-set — the bricks sit on compacted sand with little or no mortar between them. Resetting is straightforward:
  • Pry up problem bricks with a flat bar — they lift easily
  • Add or remove sand underneath to bring to correct height
  • Re-set the brick, check level with neighbors using a straight edge
  • Sweep dry sand or polymeric sand into the joints to lock everything together
Flat pry bar Polymeric sand — $20–30 Rubber mallet
DO
Clean with TSP wash, treat efflorescence with vinegar
~1.5 hrs + 48 hr dry
  • White chalky deposits (efflorescence): scrub with diluted white vinegar (1:1 with water), dwell 10 min, rinse thoroughly — do this first
  • Then TSP wash: mix per label ratio, scrub with stiff brush, rinse, allow 48 hrs of dry time
  • The brick will look dramatically different after cleaning — the color and texture reveal themselves
DO
Seal with penetrating masonry sealer only — never film-forming
~1.5 hrs + cure
A penetrating sealer soaks into the brick, binds the surface, repels moisture, and makes it cleanable — without changing appearance or creating a slipping hazard.
  • Do NOT use epoxy coating or film-forming sealer — it peels as moisture migrates through
  • Apply with a pump garden sprayer — saturate, then back-brush
  • One coat for most brick; two coats on very absorbent surfaces
  • 24 hrs before foot traffic, 72 hrs before placing heavy storage
Foundation Armor SX5000 — $60–80/gal Surecrete Eco-Finish — $45–65/gal

Storage setup — what goes where

With door, windows, and floor done, you're ready to set up storage. Bikes on the wall opposite the door, seasonal bins along the back wall, lawn equipment in the floor zone near the door for easy roll-in/roll-out.

DO
Bike hooks — mount on a board anchored to the stone
Don't try to anchor individual bike hooks directly into the stone at precise spacings. Instead:
  • Mount a 2×8 horizontal board to the wall with sleeve anchors — 3 anchor points minimum
  • Screw all bike hooks into the board at whatever spacing works for your bikes
  • Stagger heights: alternate 54" / 42" so handlebars don't clash
  • Kids bikes on lower hooks; adults on higher for easier lifting
  • Small shelf above the hooks for helmets
Rubber-coated bike wall hooks — $12–18 each 2×8 × 10 ft board ½" sleeve anchors for the board mount
DO
Freestanding metal shelving for seasonal bins — back wall
Given the stone walls, freestanding heavy-duty garage shelving is easier than wall-mounting for heavy bin loads. Position against the back wall away from the door.
  • Husky or Edsal 5-shelf steel units — 48"×18"×72"
  • Same labeled bin system as the workshop: Sterilite 27-gal for seasonal, 18-gal for smaller categories
  • Sleds and long items hang on wall hooks above the shelving
Husky 5-shelf steel unit — $65–90
DO
Lawn equipment zone — center floor near the door
Mower, hoses, pressure washer — anything you roll in and out. One clear path from the door to the equipment zone. Wall-mount a tool rack for long-handled tools (rakes, shovels) to keep them vertical and out of the walkway. Wall-mount garden tool rack — $25–40
TIP
Battery-powered motion lights until electrical is run
Before you run electrical to the basement, two battery-powered motion-sensor LED lights do the job well — one near the door, one at the back wall. When you eventually run conduit from the upper barn, you can add a proper switched fixture. The stone ceiling and exposed timber above would look stunning with a couple of cage-style bulb fixtures. Govee battery LED motion light — $20–30 each
