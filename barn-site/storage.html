Craft & Workshop

12 × 15.5 ft · Tools · Sports gear · Craft table · Workbench · Bulk storage moves to basement

✦

The updated plan — workshop, not storage

With bikes, seasonal bins, sleds, and lawn equipment moving to the basement, the rear half of the barn is freed up to become something genuinely useful: a craft room and workshop. The natural light from the back windows makes it ideal for detailed work. The partition opening stays completely clear.

What changed: The original plan had this half as mixed storage — bikes, seasonal bins, tools, overflow. Now that the basement handles bulk storage, this space has one clear purpose: active use. Workbench, craft table, tool pegboard, and sports gear only. Nothing that belongs in the basement comes up here.

🔨 Zone A — Workbench

Back wall · under the windows

An 8 ft workbench runs along the back wall directly under the large windows — the best natural light in the space. Pegboard above for tools. Clear counter surface for projects.

  • 8 ft butcher block or reclaimed slab top
  • Pegboard above — hand tools, drill bits, clamps
  • Power strip + outlets along back wall (Plan B)
  • Bin drawer or shelf below for hardware

✂️ Zone B — Craft Table

Center of room

A 6×4 ft table in the center of the space — big enough to spread out a project, close enough to the workbench to share tools. Freestanding so it can be moved if needed.

  • 6×4 ft table — IKEA RÅSKOG, folding table, or DIY
  • Chairs or stools tucked underneath when not in use
  • Good overhead lighting directly above

🔧 Zone C — Tool Storage

Left wall · above window

Pegboard on the left wall above the window for hand tools. Shelving below the window for power tools, the pressure washer, extension cords, and hardware.

  • 4×4 ft pegboard panel above window
  • Shelf below window for power tools
  • Pressure washer + hose reel
  • Small parts organizer for fasteners

⚽ Zone D — Sports Gear

Left wall · below window + near door

Open ball bin and gear hooks near the single door — grab it on the way out. The stuff that gets used most often should be closest to the exit.

  • Open-top ball bin at floor level
  • Hooks for gear bags + rackets
  • Cleats / sports shoes shelf
  • Right next to the single door

📚 Zone E — Craft Shelving

Right wall · full height

The solid right wall (no windows, no door) is the best shelving wall in the space. Floor-to-ceiling shelving for craft supplies, labeled bins, and project materials.

  • Adjustable shelving — IVAR or reclaimed
  • Labeled bins for craft supplies by category
  • Visible from partition opening — keep it tidy
  • Built-in bookcase (existing) near partition top-left
The partition opening stays completely clear. No furniture, no shelving, nothing blocking the threshold between the entertaining space and the workshop. This is a through-traffic corridor — treat it like a hallway.

The sort session — decide what stays up here vs goes downstairs

Before building anything, everything comes out and gets sorted into four piles. The key question for each item: does it belong in the active workshop upstairs, or in the basement with the bulk storage?

Trash / toss

Broken, expired, rusted through, unused in 3+ years. Be ruthless — you cannot organize clutter and this space has a clear purpose now.

Donate / sell

Working but no longer needed. Kids' outgrown bikes move fast on Facebook Marketplace. Duplicate tools too.

Workshop (upstairs)

Hand tools, power tools, sports gear, craft supplies, active project materials. Stuff you use regularly and want quick access to.

Basement

Bikes, sleds, seasonal bins, lawn equipment, hoses. Anything bulky or infrequently accessed. If you reach for it less than once a month, it lives downstairs.

DO
Be strict about the upstairs/basement divide
The most common mistake is letting the workshop fill up with things that should live in the basement. Set a rule now: if it's seasonal, bulky, or used less than monthly, it goes downstairs. The workshop only contains things you'd reasonably reach for while working on a project or grabbing gear for an activity.
TIP
Sort craft supplies into categories during the sort — not later
If you're setting up a craft space, label your bins during the sort while you have everything out and in hand. Categories to consider:
  • Fabric / sewing
  • Paper / cards / scrapbooking
  • Paint + brushes
  • Seasonal / holiday crafts
  • Kids' craft supplies
  • Hardware / fasteners (tools zone)
!
Assess the existing built-in bookcase now
The built-in bookcase near the partition opening is staying. While everything is out, check it properly: any rotted shelves, loose joints, pest damage? Clean it thoroughly with TSP (same as the wall cleaning), let dry, then seal with the same interior stain you used on the rest of the barn. It'll look intentional rather than abandoned.

Building the workbench — Zone A, back wall

An 8 ft workbench under the back windows. Same basic construction as the bar — ledger board into studs, front legs, top surface. But this one is built for work: a bit lower (34–36"), deeper (24–28"), and tougher.

DO
Set workbench height at 34–36" — lower than the bar
Bar height (42") is for standing and drinking. Workbench height (34–36") is for standing and working — more leverage, less fatigue. The right height is roughly elbow height minus 2–4 inches. If you're ~6 ft, 36" is comfortable for most work.
  • Standard workbench: 34–36" (adjust to your height)
  • Depth: 24–28" — deeper than the bar, gives more project space
  • Width: 8 ft running the full back wall
DO
Build the frame — ledger into studs, front legs, lower shelf
~3–4 hrs
Same approach as the bar frame. Locate studs in the back wall first. Mount a 2×6 ledger at 32–34" (the top surface adds 1.5–2"). Front legs of 4×4 or doubled 2×4. Lower horizontal rail connecting legs — this becomes a bottom shelf for bins, power tools, or the pressure washer.
  • Pre-drill into reclaimed lumber to avoid splitting
  • Check level on the ledger before final tightening — everything follows from it
  • 3 front legs max across 8 ft — at each end and one center
  • Lower shelf rail at 6–8" off the floor — keeps things off the ground
2×6 ledger board 4×4 front legs 3" structural screws
DO
Top surface — thick and tough
The workbench top takes more abuse than the bar. Options from most to least durable:

Option 1 — 2×4 or 2×6 edge-glued slab: Glue up a series of 2×4s on edge to create a 3.5"-thick top. Extremely strong, completely DIY from construction lumber, looks great in a rustic space. ~$40–60 in materials.

Option 2 — Butcher block (same as bar): Consistent look if you used it on the bar. Easy to source, easy to work with. May need replacement eventually if heavily abused. ~$80–150.

Option 3 — Reclaimed wide plank: Whatever you have. If thick enough (1.5"+) it works fine. Free if you have the lumber.

Finish with a penetrating oil (not poly) — it's easier to touch up as the surface gets scratched and dinged over time. Watco Danish Oil — $20–30 Or leave raw + wax coat
TIP
Add a power strip along the back wall now
Even before you have proper electrical, a heavy-duty outdoor-rated extension cord running from the barn's existing outlet (or a temporary power source) to a 6-outlet power strip mounted under the upper shelf of the workbench gives you immediate usability for power tools, chargers, and a work light. When proper electrical gets run (Plan B), you replace the extension cord with a hard-wired circuit — the strip stays. Tripp Lite 6-outlet heavy-duty power strip — $25–35

Tool wall — Zone C, left wall above window

A 4×4 ft pegboard panel above the left wall window puts every hand tool in plain sight and within reach. Below the window: a single deep shelf for power tools and bulky equipment. Simple, cheap, and highly functional.

DO
Mount the pegboard panel on furring strips — not flat to the wall
~1 hr
Pegboard hooks need about ½–¾" of clearance behind the board to hook in and out. On a flat barn board wall, you need to mount the pegboard on 1×2 or 1×3 furring strips first, which creates that gap.
  • Run two horizontal furring strips across the wall — one near the top, one near the bottom
  • Screw furring strips into studs with 2.5" screws
  • Mount the pegboard panel to the furring strips
  • Paint the pegboard and the wall behind it the same color — the gap becomes invisible and the tools pop
4×4 ft pegboard panel — $20–30 1×3 furring strips Pegboard hook assortment — $15–25
DO
Install one deep shelf below the window for power tools
~45 min
A single 2×12 board on heavy-duty brackets, positioned just below the window sill, holds the drill, circular saw, jigsaw, sander, and the pressure washer. Keep it at a height where you can see everything on it at a glance — around 18–24" off the floor is right.
  • 2×12 or doubled 2×10 for the depth — power tools need room
  • Heavy-duty shelf brackets rated for 100+ lbs
  • Screws into studs — not just into barn board
Simpson Strong-Tie heavy-duty bracket — $8–14 each
TIP
Use the French cleat system here too if you built it for the bar
A horizontal French cleat running across the left wall above the window lets you hang pegboard, shelves, tool holders, and hooks all off the same system — and reposition everything without drilling new holes. If you're already cutting cleats for the bar shelving, cut a few extra for the workshop wall.
DO
Add a small parts organizer for fasteners and hardware
A wall-mounted parts cabinet or a set of stackable drawer units on the shelf keeps screws, nails, drill bits, and hardware from becoming a loose pile. This is one of those small investments that saves significant time over the course of a project. Akro-Mils wall-mount bin rack — $30–50 Or Stanley wall-mount organizer — $25–40

Sports zone — Zone D, left wall near the door

The stuff that gets grabbed on the way out the door — balls, gear bags, rackets, cleats. Positioned right next to the single door so it takes seconds to grab and go. No bins with lids here: everything open and visible.

DO
Open ball bin at floor level — the most-used item in any sports zone
A large open-top wire or canvas bin for balls is the single most useful piece of sports storage. No lid, no latches — just drop balls in, grab them out. Position it right beside the door.
  • Wire mesh bin or heavy canvas tote — $15–35
  • Or DIY: a simple 18"×18" wooden crate from reclaimed lumber, open top
  • Large enough for soccer balls, basketballs, footballs simultaneously
Rubbermaid large utility bin — $20–30 Or DIY reclaimed wood crate
DO
Gear bag hooks at 60–66" — reachable but out of the way
3–4 heavy coat hooks on a mounted board at around 60–66" height hold gear bags, backpacks, and racket bags. Mount on a board anchored to studs — not direct to barn board — so the hooks can carry real weight.
  • Mount a 2×6 board horizontally at 60–66" height, screwed into studs
  • Space hooks 12–14" apart
  • Heavy cast-iron coat hooks look great and hold up — $8–15 each
DO
One shelf at 36–42" for cleats and sports shoes
A single 2×8 shelf below the hooks at 36–42" keeps sports shoes off the floor and in view. Open shelves work better than bins for shoes — you can see everything at a glance and grab without rummaging.
DO
Vertical rack for rackets, bats, and sticks
Long-handled sports equipment (tennis/badminton rackets, baseball bats, hockey sticks, lacrosse sticks) stores best vertically. A simple PVC pipe or wooden dowel holder mounted on the wall — or a floor-standing rack — keeps everything upright and contained without taking up floor space. Wallmaster vertical sports rack — $25–40 Or DIY: two horizontal boards with holes drilled for dowels
5

Craft shelving — Zone E, right wall

The right wall is the best shelving wall in the space — solid, no windows, no door, full 12 ft length. Floor-to-ceiling adjustable shelving for craft supplies, project bins, and the labeled tote system. This wall is partially visible through the partition opening, so it should look intentional.

DO
Use adjustable shelving — IVAR or equivalent
Craft supply needs change over time. Adjustable shelving lets you reconfigure as projects evolve without rebuilding. IKEA IVAR (pine, fully adjustable) stained to match the barn interior looks completely at home here. Or build reclaimed wood shelves on a French cleat system for the same flexibility.
  • IVAR unit (19×70") — $60–90 stained to match
  • Or 2–3 runs of 1×10 reclaimed boards on French cleats
  • Anchor the top to the wall regardless of system — anti-tip with kids around
  • Match the stain to the rest of the interior or go slightly lighter for a built-in feel
IKEA IVAR 19×70" — $65 Anti-tip strap — $10
DO
Use matching bins throughout — same brand, same color
This shelf is visible from the entertaining space. Matching bins make it look designed, not accumulated. Sterilite or HDX clear or gray totes in consistent sizes, labeled with a P-Touch label maker, will look clean and organized from across the room.
  • Sterilite 6-qt for small craft supplies
  • Sterilite 18-gal for larger project materials
  • Label front face and short end of every bin
  • Group by category, not by size
Brother P-Touch label maker — $25 Sterilite clear bins — $5–12 each
TIP
Put a chalkboard or whiteboard above the craft table
A simple 2×3 ft chalkboard or whiteboard mounted on the wall above the craft table or near the workbench is one of those things that gets used constantly — project notes, measurements, shopping lists, to-do items. A framed chalkboard in a barn setting looks great and costs $20–30. VersaChalk framed chalkboard — $25–40 Or DIY: chalkboard paint on a reclaimed board with a frame
